Intelligence is a lever.
Not a compass.

For much of history, the ability to reason, create, and solve difficult problems has brought economic value and social distinction. AI is changing that. As competent intellectual work becomes cheaper to produce, thriving will require more than knowing how to use powerful tools. It will require knowing what those tools are for.

01 / CHOOSE WELL

Cultivate judgment,
not just intelligence.

Intelligence helps solve problems. Judgment helps identify which problems deserve solving. Wisdom asks whether solving them will make life better.

When answers are plentiful, the ability to ask worthwhile questions, recognize quality, and distinguish evidence from plausibility becomes more valuable. A model might help you build a profitable business. It cannot settle whether that business is worth the life you would spend building it.

Put it into practice

For one consequential decision, write down your objective, the trade-offs, and what would change your mind. Ask AI to challenge your reasoning—not merely endorse it.

Know what matters, not just how to get it.
02 / KEEP GROWING

Preserve the friction
that develops you.

Some difficulty is waste. Some is how understanding and mastery develop. Reading a compelling argument is not the same as learning to construct one; receiving a solution is not equivalent to understanding it.

Use AI to remove drudgery, but resist delegating every experience that builds your competence. When the stakes matter, you should understand enough to recognize when an elegant answer is wrong.

Put it into practice

Keep one recurring unassisted practice—writing, reasoning, coding, or another skill you value. Try first; then use AI for critique, explanation, and improvement.

Outsource drudgery, not development.
03 / CREATE REAL VALUE

Own problems,
not just tasks.

As execution gets cheaper, value may shift toward identifying real needs, navigating constraints, delivering reliable outcomes, and accepting responsibility. Generating code is not the same as solving a user's problem. Producing a strategy is not the same as making it work.

Build transferable expertise, relationships, and a record of useful results. Where feasible, pursue ownership and financial resilience rather than depending entirely on selling your time. No occupation is guaranteed immunity from change.

And remember: rising productivity does not automatically mean shared prosperity. Ownership, competition, and institutions shape who benefits. The future is something to help shape, not just adapt to.

Put it into practice

Talk to someone with an actual problem. Build or improve one useful thing, test whether it helps, and measure the outcome rather than the amount of work produced.

Be accountable for outcomes, not impressed by output.
04 / LIVE IN THE WORLD

Invest in reality,
relationships, and attention.

When polished content becomes effortless to generate, evidence and follow-through matter more. Test ideas against firsthand observations. Build trust by keeping promises and showing up when it is inconvenient.

Make space for the physical world: exercise, cook, walk, make things, and spend time with people face-to-face. These are not merely defenses against automation. They are part of living well.

Guard your attention against infinite personalized stimulation. Deep work, boredom, conversation, and unmediated experience need room to exist.

Put it into practice

Schedule a meaningful conversation or shared activity, and protect at least one uninterrupted block for reading, creating, or simply being present.

Don't mistake consuming life for living it.
05 / REMEMBER WHY

Anchor your worth
beyond productivity.

If your identity depends on being unusually intelligent or useful, increasingly capable machines can feel like a threat to your worth. But human value is not identical to economic output.

An AI might compose better music than you. That does not diminish your experience of learning an instrument. It might write more beautifully. That does not make finding your own words for someone you love meaningless.

Distinguish instrumental goods—money, status, efficiency—from goods worth pursuing for their own sake: friendship, understanding, beauty, love, and meaningful activity. Ambition matters, but it should serve a life you actually want.

Put it into practice

Choose one activity for its intrinsic value, with no plan to monetize, post, optimize, or turn it into an achievement.

Your worth is not a comparison with a machine.

A final thought

From doing more
to living better.

The intelligence age may help us reduce drudgery and expand what is possible. But greater capability does not automatically produce a better life. We can become more productive without becoming happier, and more informed without becoming wiser.

The opportunity is to use these gains to make room for what matters—not merely to raise expectations and demand more output from ourselves.

The goal is not to become irreplaceable. It is to build a life worth living, even when much of what you can do can also be done by machines.

Let intelligence become a lever, not a compass.
A life is not a problem to be optimized to completion. It is something to be lived.
